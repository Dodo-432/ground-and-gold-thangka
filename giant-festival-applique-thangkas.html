Giant Festival Thangkas: The Appliqué Banners Unveiled Once a Year

Imagine standing on a dark hillside before sunrise, surrounded by thousands of people in their warmest clothes, many of them holding prayer beads and murmuring mantras. A long roll of cloth is carried up the slope on the shoulders of monks. As the sky lightens, the cloth is hauled up and unrolled down a wall or a hillside, and a Buddha several stories tall appears above the crowd. Then, a few hours later, it is rolled up and carried away again until next year.

These are the giant festival thangkas of the Himalayan world. They are among the largest religious images made anywhere, and they are not painted at all. Most are built from pieces of silk sewn together in appliqué. Here is what they are, how they are made, where they are shown, and how to be a respectful visitor if you are lucky enough to see one.

Goku and thongdrel: the names

In Tibetan these great images are often called goku (also written gö ku), which can be translated as "cloth image" or "silk image." In Bhutan the large unveiled image is called a thongdrel, a word usually explained as "liberation on sight." The idea behind that name is shared across the region: simply seeing such an image with faith is believed to plant a powerful seed of merit, and for some, to help bring liberation.

That belief is part of why the unveiling draws such crowds. People come not only to look but to receive a blessing, and many families travel long distances to be there.

How appliqué thangkas are made

A giant thangka begins much like a painted one, with a carefully proportioned drawing. Because of the scale, the design is worked out in sections. Each colored area of the image, a robe, a face, a lotus petal, a cloud, is cut from silk or brocade in the right color and shade. The pieces are then stitched onto a backing so that together they form the complete picture.

Outlines, shading and detail

Fine lines that a painter would draw with a brush are made with cord in appliqué. A common technique uses a thin core, traditionally horsehair is often mentioned, wrapped in silk thread and couched down along the edges of each shape. This gives the image crisp, raised outlines visible from far away. Shading is suggested by placing pieces of slightly different colors side by side, and small details may be embroidered. Faces are given particular care, since the eyes and expression are what thousands of people will look at.

The work requires a team of skilled tailors and embroiderers, often working for a long time. The same patience and grounding in proportion that painters learn appears here too, and the long apprenticeship behind thangka painting has its parallel in the workshops of appliqué makers, who learn by copying and assisting masters before they handle a central figure.

How appliqué differs from painted thangkas

The difference is not only scale. A painted thangka uses mineral pigments and fine brushwork on prepared cotton, and it can show delicate shading and tiny ornamental patterns. Appliqué works with flat areas of fabric and cord, so its language is bolder: clear shapes, strong contrasts, and lines that read from a distance. Smaller appliqué thangkas exist too, but the technique is especially suited to images meant to be seen by a crowd.

Fabric also behaves differently. Silk has a sheen that changes with the angle of light, so an appliqué image shimmers in the early morning in a way paint does not. It is also lighter and more flexible than a painted canvas of that size would be, which makes it possible to roll, carry and hang. Painted thangkas, by contrast, can play with ground colors and gold line on a small scale, as in black, gold and red ground thangkas, which depend on detail that would be lost on a hillside.

Famous unveiling festivals

Many monasteries in Tibet, Bhutan, Ladakh and the wider Himalayan region keep a giant thangka. A few unveilings are especially well known.

  • Tashilhunpo, Shigatse. The monastery has a tall stone wall built for displaying giant thangkas. During a summer festival, large images of Buddhas are hung on it over several days, and the wall is visible across the town.
  • Drepung and the Shoton festival, Lhasa. Shoton, often called the Yogurt Festival, begins with the unveiling of a huge thangka on the hillside at Drepung Monastery at dawn. Crowds climb up in the dark to be there when it appears.
  • Bhutan's tsechu festivals. At several tsechus, including the well-known festival at Paro, a thongdrel is unveiled before sunrise on the final day. The Paro thongdrel shows Guru Rinpoche, the teacher credited with bringing Buddhism to the Himalayas.
  • Hemis, Ladakh. Hemis Monastery displays its largest thangka only once every twelve years, which makes those years especially significant.

Other monasteries, such as Labrang in Amdo, have their own unveilings, often tied to New Year or other major observances. Dates follow the Tibetan or Bhutanese lunar calendar, so they shift from year to year on the Western calendar.

Why at dawn, and why so briefly

Most giant thangkas are unveiled early, often before sunrise, and taken down within a few hours. There are practical and spiritual reasons, and people explain them in different ways. Practically, strong sunlight fades silk and dyes, and these images are irreplaceable treasures that a monastery may have kept for generations. Limiting exposure helps them last.

Spiritually, the first light of day is an auspicious time, and the brevity of the display makes it an event. A sacred image seen for a few hours once a year is something people plan their year around. That rarity is part of its power.

Storage and carrying

When it is not on display, a giant thangka is rolled or folded and kept in a monastery storeroom, protected from damp, pests and light. On the day of the unveiling, monks carry it in procession, often on their shoulders, sometimes with music from horns and drums. Raising it is a team effort with ropes and many hands, and so is the careful rolling at the end, which keeps the silk from creasing or tearing.

Old giant thangkas are sometimes repaired or replaced when they wear out, and making a new one is a major undertaking for a monastery and its supporters.

Visiting etiquette

If you travel to see an unveiling, remember that you are attending a religious event, not a show. A few simple courtesies go a long way.

  • Arrive early and quietly, and expect a crowd. Dress modestly and warmly.
  • Follow local rules on photography. Some places restrict photos or drones, and flash is often unwelcome.
  • Do not block the view or the path of pilgrims. When walking around the site, go clockwise, as local people do.
  • If people are touching their heads to the edge of the cloth or offering white scarves, watch and follow guidance rather than pushing forward.
  • Do not climb on walls or stand above the image.

Frequently asked questions

Are giant thangkas painted or sewn?

Most are appliqué, built from cut silk pieces stitched onto a backing, with corded outlines and some embroidery.

Can visitors attend the unveilings?

Many unveilings are open to the public, though travel rules in some regions mean visitors need permits or a guided tour. Check current requirements before planning a trip.

Why is the image taken down so quickly?

To protect fragile silk from sunlight, and because the short, rare display is part of its sacred character.

A once-a-year encounter

Giant festival thangkas turn a sacred image into a shared event. The cold climb, the slow procession, the moment of unrolling and the quiet devotion of the crowd are all part of what the image means.

Even if you never stand on that hillside at dawn, knowing how these banners are made and shown deepens your view of thangka art as a whole. Some are meant for a quiet room; these are meant for a whole community at once.
