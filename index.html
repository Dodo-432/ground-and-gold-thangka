Look closer at how a thangka is made

Ground & Gold is about the craft side of thangka painting: the ground a painting is laid on, the tools in the painter's hand, the landscape behind the deity, and the training it takes to paint any of it well.

The craft is part of the meaning

A thangka is a devotional object first, but it is also a feat of craftsmanship. The choice of a black or gold ground changes the mood of a painting. A single cloud can take an afternoon of shading. Knowing how these things are done does not take away from the sacred side of the work; it helps you see how much care went into it.

What we look at

Painted grounds and line work. The brushes, shells and burnishing stones of a working studio. The clouds, peaks and rivers that frame each figure. The giant appliqué banners unrolled on festival mornings. And the long apprenticeship behind every confident line.

For eyes, not experts

You do not need to know Tibetan or art history to enjoy these notes. Each piece starts from what you can actually see on the canvas and builds from there.

Slow looking

Our advice for any thangka is the same: give it ten quiet minutes. Start at the edges, follow the landscape inward, notice where the gold sits. The paintings were made slowly, and they reward the same pace.
